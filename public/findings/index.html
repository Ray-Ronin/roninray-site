WHAT THE DATA SHOWS

The patterns in the evidence. Each one gives the finding, the evidence, where it comes from, and why it matters.

TESTS AND PEOPLE CHECK DIFFERENT THINGS

FINDING
Tests can tell me the ball moves like the arcade’s. They can’t tell me if the game is fair or fun. When I played it I found problems the tests had no way to see.
EVIDENCE
A scripted player beat level 1 by 9–2 at S6. Ronin lost to the same level 4–9 at CP-C. None of the 22 changes on the chart touched the file with the ball physics. It was last changed on Oct 18, before the first play-test. The physics test numbers stayed at their baseline through the S4b, S6b, S8b and S12b–e fix-ups.
SOURCE
docs/plan.md Session log; the game’s git history; the play-test chart.
WHY IT MATTERS
This goes to my third smaller question. The tests answered “is it correct?” and only a person could answer “is it right?”. I needed both.

ONE PLAY-TEST MOVED THE NUMBERS MOST

FINDING
The CP-C play-test changed the win rates far more than anything after it. The later changes were small, and some of them might be noise.
EVIDENCE
At level 1, the same robot went from 34% against S6 to 78% against S6b, up 44 points. After that, S12c moved levels 1, 3 and 4 by 5–7 points and S12d moved nothing more than 2. S12e moved up to 3 points, and it only made the words on the wall stay up longer. It did shift the frame numbers the CPU’s choices are seeded from. After S6b, Ronin said levels 1, 5 and 9 feel clearly different. At CP-C they had felt about the same.
SOURCE
docs/decisions.md “CPU difficulty” and “Hitting”; docs/plan.md Session log; the win-rate chart.
WHY IT MATTERS
S12e didn’t change how the game plays and the numbers still moved 3 points. So I don’t trust a change of 3 points or less to mean anything, and that includes the gap between S12c and S12d.

THE LEVELS ARE IN ORDER, BUT THE STEPS AREN’T EVEN

FINDING
Every level is harder than the one before it, so the order is right. But the jump from 5 to 6 is much bigger than the jumps at the top.
EVIDENCE
Every measured curve falls at every level, and S6b, S12c, S12d and S12e all meet the plan’s three targets. In S12d the steps run from 3 to 20 points. The step from level 5 to 6 was 14 points in S6b, 18 in S12c, 20 in S12d and 16 in S12e. From level 7 to 8 to 9 the steps are 4 and 3. The docs left the 5 to 6 step “for the owner to judge in play”. When Ronin plays it, level 6 feels much harder than level 5. Volleys also start at level 6, but no test checked whether that causes the jump.
SOURCE
docs/decisions.md “CPU difficulty” and “Hitting”; docs/plan.md Session log.
WHY IT MATTERS
A “smooth” difficulty curve was what I wanted, and this one is smooth in order but not in size. The top levels are also hard to compare, because a robot that barely wins has little room left to fall.

THE PHYSICS IS EXACT BETWEEN HITS

FINDING
Between racket hits, my physics matches the recordings exactly. The weak spot is the moment the racket hits the ball.
EVIDENCE
All 458 flights (164 + 294) match on every frame. The golden test matches 99.3% and 99.4% of frames, and its 81 and 111 misses equal the 81 and 111 racket hits. On its own, the racket-hit formula matches 67 of 81 and 73 of 111 hits. Those counts include the 24 serves that the table on the Research page leaves out.
SOURCE
test/baselines.json; Research → How close is the match?; the physics chart.
WHY IT MATTERS
This is my best answer to “how accurately can it be rebuilt?”. It’s exact where the code could be read and tested, and less exact where the arcade does extra things to a hit that decompiling didn’t fully explain.

THE CLOSEST MATCH WASN’T ALWAYS THE BEST GAME

FINDING
I changed the drop shot so it no longer matches the arcade, on purpose, because the game played better. Fixing it also moved part of the problem somewhere else.
EVIDENCE
Second bounces past the short line fell from about 70% to 0–6%, and tins from about 22% to 8%. But about 14% of drops now fall short of the front wall, so all lost together stayed about the same, at 21–25%. The first cap the plan tried still left 25–40% past the line. After S6b, Ronin called the drop “perfect”.
SOURCE
docs/decisions.md “Hitting”; docs/plan.md Session log; the drop chart.
WHY IT MATTERS
My goal was to play like the arcade, and here I chose not to. A measurement can show what a change costs, but deciding if the cost is worth it was my call.

ABOUT HALF OF WHAT I CHECKED STAYED THE SAME

FINDING
Play-testing wasn’t only finding problems. About half of it was deciding that something was already right, and the changes were mostly about how the game feels, looks and sounds.
EVIDENCE
Counted from the notes, 22 points asked for a change and 21 were kept. Eight fix-up sessions followed, and S12b came from a request between checkpoints. CP-E needed no fix-up session. CP-F took three rounds of fixes, and its last pass found nothing.
SOURCE
docs/plan.md and docs/plan-v2.md; docs/decisions.md; the feedback table; the play-test chart.
WHY IT MATTERS
AI wrote every fix, but it didn’t pick them. Saying “that’s fine, leave it” was a decision too. These counts are my own, and CP-G has 10 of the kept points by itself, so they show a rough pattern and not a real measurement.

ANSWERS TO MY QUESTIONS

Each answer is my conclusion. The evidence under it is what you can check for yourself.

1. HOW ACCURATELY CAN A 1992 ARCADE GAME’S RULES AND PHYSICS BE REBUILT?

MY ANSWER
Very accurately for the ball in flight, less exactly at the moment the racket hits it, and not at all for the parts of the game my robot never reached.
EVIDENCE
All 458 recorded flights match on every frame. The golden test matches 99.3% and 99.4% of frames, and its misses are the racket hits. Some rules, like the tin and out-line checks, weren’t found in the code. See The physics is exact between hits and The arcade data comes from one ROM version.

2. WHAT DID AI DO WELL, AND HOW WERE ITS MISTAKES CAUGHT?

MY ANSWER
AI was good at work with a right answer you can test, like matching the arcade’s numbers. Its first guesses about how a game should play were often off. Those were caught by measuring or by me playing, not by the AI noticing.
EVIDENCE
The plan’s first level table gave level 5 a 31% win rate, under its target. Its first cap on the drop shot still left 25–40% past the short line. The first test robot won only 19%. This site’s build log also said the physics matched “with zero error”, which overstated the 99.3% golden test, and it was corrected on Nov 8. The AI also wrote the tests, so a test could share the code’s mistake. See How AI work was checked and The win rates come from the project’s own script.

3. WHICH DECISIONS NEEDED A PERSON, AND HOW DID PLAY-TESTING CHANGE THE GAME?

MY ANSWER
Anything about whether the game is fair, fun or right needed a person: how hard level 1 should be, when to leave the arcade’s numbers behind, and when to stop. Play-testing changed how the game plays, looks and sounds, but it didn’t touch the ball physics.
EVIDENCE
By my count, 22 play-test points asked for a change, 21 were kept, and eight fix-up sessions followed. The corner shape, the racket shift and the wider court were each picked by trying options in the game. See Tests and people check different things, About half of what I checked stayed the same and the feedback table.

4. WHAT ARE THE ETHICAL LIMITS OF REVERSE ENGINEERING AND AI-MADE ART AND SOUND?

MY ANSWER
I started with a simple line: study how the game works, but don’t take its art, sound or name. I still think that line is right, but it’s less clear than I thought. My physics is a port of the arcade’s math, and AI art raises questions I can’t fully answer about what it learned from.
EVIDENCE
See Ethics for what was done and which questions are still open, and Other perspectives for how others see it.

5. WHAT DID I LEARN BY BUILDING A GAME WITH AI THAT I WOULDN’T HAVE LEARNED WITHOUT IT?

MY ANSWER
It is actually not that hard to make a game with AI. All you need is an idea and a little creativity.
EVIDENCE
What the record can show: every decision and play-test note is dated in the build log. It can’t show what was learned. One study found students who used a plain AI tool for practice did worse on a later exam without it, so this question needs a real answer.

MY GUIDING QUESTION: HOW CAN AI TOOLS HELP ONE STUDENT RESEARCH, DESIGN AND BUILD A COMPLETE VIDEO GAME?

AI tools can do most of the making: reading and rebuilding old code, writing the game, and producing first versions of art and sound. That let one student finish something that would normally need a team. But AI couldn’t decide what the game should be, or tell whether it was right. It helped most when I set the goal, tested the result and made the calls. So my answer is that AI can do the work, but a person still has to direct it and check it. It also has costs: I didn’t write the code myself, and this is one student and one project, so it doesn’t show how it would go for anyone else.

Evidence: research began on Oct 18 and version 1.0 was released on Oct 23, 2026. The rest is linked in the answers above and in Limitations.

LIMITATIONS

What this evidence can and can’t show.

THE TEST PLAYER IS A BOT

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS
The reference beginner always reacts in 15 frames and never learns or gets tired. It was checked against one result: it wins 34% of points against the S6 level 1, and Ronin won 4 of 13 points (31%) in one match. A first version won only 19%, so it was changed. The log itself says “beatable at level 1” rests on a player calibrated to that one result.
WHAT THIS MEANS
The win-rate chart shows how the levels compare for one robot. It doesn’t show what a real beginner would score. One 13-point match is very little to calibrate it with, and I don’t know if real beginners play like it.

THE PLAY-TESTERS WERE THE PEOPLE BUILDING IT

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS
Me and my dad have been play-testing it as we were making it, but my friends have play-tested it once recently. They had a hard time with the game and didn’t know what to do or how to select, and gave advice like “You should add abilities to each character.” Me and my dad have been changing things as we play-test, but I haven’t changed anything after my friends gave feedback.
WHAT THIS MEANS
It matters because we know all the controls and everything else about the game. It tells us that we need to make things clearer and more simple. I didn’t add character abilities yet because I was doing other things for the game and wanted to do that when I had different characters and when I will give them different stats.

THE WIN RATES COME FROM THE PROJECT’S OWN SCRIPT

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS
The numbers come from test/measure-levels.js, which is in the game’s repo and is run by hand. It isn’t part of the automatic tests. Claude Code wrote it, as it wrote the game. The levels were also tuned against the same robot: the plan’s first table gave level 5 31%, under its target, so the table was changed. Fixed seeds make a run repeatable. The latest measurement is S12e, on Oct 23.
WHAT THIS MEANS
Repeatable doesn’t mean right, because a bug in the script would repeat too. And since the levels were adjusted until they met the targets, meeting them doesn’t prove much by itself. Nobody outside the project has checked this, though the code is public, so anyone could. By my calculation, the usual margin-of-error formula gives about 3 points for 1,000 rallies near 50%, which fits the 3 points S12e moved.

THE ARCADE DATA COMES FROM ONE ROM VERSION

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS
All the arcade numbers come from one set in the MAME emulator: squash, World version 1.0. Other sets exist, such as USA version 1.1, and they weren’t compared. The two recordings were made in an emulator, not on a real machine, by a robot that pressed buttons at random and never won, so later rounds weren’t seen. Ghidra didn’t find some rules, like the tin and out-line checks.
WHAT THIS MEANS
“Matches the arcade” really means it matches this one version, as an emulator runs it, in the parts of the game my robot reached. I can’t say anything about other versions or about parts of the game that weren’t recorded.

WHAT’S MISSING

  • S6’s win rates at levels 2 to 9, and how many rallies the 34% at level 1 used.
  • How many drops fell short of the front wall before S6b, and the total lost before S6b as its own number.
  • Any person’s measured win rate after CP-C. The later notes say how the levels felt, not what the score was.

OTHER PERSPECTIVES

People who know this field disagree about projects like this one. Each card keeps what they said or measured apart from my own opinion.

GAME DEVELOPERS

THE EVIDENCE
In a 2026 survey of more than 2,300 game industry professionals, 52% said generative AI is having a negative impact on the industry, up from 30% and then 18% in the two years before. About 7% said positive, and 36% use it in their job. Among game programmers, 59% were negative (Elderkin).
HOW FAR TO TRUST IT
A large industry survey, reported by the conference that runs it. It measures opinions about the whole industry, in a year of layoffs, not about student projects.
MY VIEW
Many professionals would be skeptical of this project, and they have reasons. Nobody lost a job over it, but the same tools might do that somewhere else.

ARTISTS

THE EVIDENCE
Visual and technical artists were the most negative group in the same survey, at 64% (Elderkin). The U.S. Copyright Office says AI output can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined enough of its expression, and that prompts alone aren’t enough (“Copyright Office Releases”). ElevenLabs says it fine-tuned its sound model on Shutterstock’s licensed tracks (“AI Generated”).
HOW FAR TO TRUST IT
“Ethically-sourced” is the companies’ own description, not an outside check. It also covers fine-tuning, and doesn’t say what else the model learned from. What SpriteCook learned from wasn’t checked for this project.
MY VIEW
I used AI for art and sound instead of paying an artist. For a school project with no money that felt fine, but it’s the same thing artists are worried about, so I don’t think I can just dismiss it.

THE PEOPLE WHO MADE THE ORIGINAL

THE EVIDENCE
Squash is still being sold: it’s in the Gaelco Sports Collection, which launched on Oct 22, 2026, four days after this project’s research began (Moyse). The 1992 game’s credits name Luis Jonama (director), Esteve Polls and Xavi Fradera (graphics) and Alexander Ekjanov (software). The project’s records show no contact with Gaelco.
HOW FAR TO TRUST IT
Nobody has asked them, so what they would think isn’t known.
MY VIEW
If I had made a game and someone rebuilt how it plays, I might be flattered or I might be annoyed. Crediting them here is the least I can do, but it isn’t the same as asking.

PEOPLE WHO PRESERVE OLD GAMES

THE EVIDENCE
A 2023 study by the Video Game History Foundation and the Software Preservation Network found that 87% of classic games released in the U.S. are out of print, with a margin of error of ±2.5%. It says piracy is often the easiest way, or the only way, to play many of them (Salvador).
HOW FAR TO TRUST IT
A random sample of 1,500 games with its method published. But the foundation argues for preservation, and the data stops in April 2023. It also doesn’t quite fit here, because Squash is in print again.
MY VIEW
Studying old games matters, and emulators make it possible. But I can’t use “the game is lost” as my reason, because this one isn’t.

TEACHERS AND STUDENTS

THE EVIDENCE
In late 2023, a quarter of U.S. public K-12 teachers said AI tools do more harm than good in K-12 education, 6% said more good than harm, 32% said an equal mix, and 35% weren’t sure (Lin). In 2025, 54% of U.S. teens had used chatbots for schoolwork, 10% for all or most of it, and 59% thought AI cheating is a regular thing at their school (McClain et al.). In one experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey, those using plain ChatGPT did 48% better in practice but 17% worse on a later exam without it. A version built to give hints scored about the same as students with no AI (Basiouny).
HOW FAR TO TRUST IT
Pew’s surveys are large and careful, but the teacher data is the oldest here. The experiment was one subject at one school, and the Wharton article describes an early version of the paper.
MY VIEW
Using AI openly and saying so is different from cheating. But the math study still worries me, because getting a good result fast doesn’t prove I learned how to do it.

SQUASH PLAYERS AND MISSION SUCCESS

THE EVIDENCE
None yet. The game is made for Hogg and the Mission Success squash program, but the project’s records show no feedback from their players. One coach has agreed to be a character in the game, which isn’t the same as reviewing it.
HOW FAR TO TRUST IT
This is the biggest gap in this section, since these are the people the game is for.
MY VIEW
I think they would care most about whether it feels like squash. I should ask them instead of guessing.

ETHICS

What was done, the question it raises, and what I think. I’m not a lawyer, and no lawyer has reviewed this project, so the legal parts are what I read, not legal advice.

COPYRIGHT AND THE ORIGINAL GAME

WHAT WAS DONE
The ROM, its graphics and its sounds are Gaelco’s copyrighted work. They were studied for reference, kept out of the public code, and never shipped or traced. The AI art and sound tools were given nothing from the arcade game. The game doesn’t use Gaelco’s name or logo; this site names them to give credit.
THE QUESTION
The game does ship more than an idea. Its physics file is a port of the arcade’s routines, using its shot tables and numbers, and the public code includes the recorded ball data used for testing. The project’s rule that “recreating gameplay mechanics is fine” is its own position, not a ruling.
MY VIEW
Ronin: say where the ROM file came from, and whether you’re comfortable with that. Then: I think taking the art would clearly be wrong and copying how a ball bounces is much closer to fine, but a port sits somewhere in between.

REVERSE ENGINEERING

WHAT WAS DONE
The arcade’s program was decompiled with Ghidra to read its rules (reverse engineering), and parts that Gaelco had encrypted were decrypted to read them. See Research → Methods.
THE QUESTION
In Sega v. Accolade (1992), a U.S. appeals court ruled that disassembling a program for a legitimate reason, with no other way to reach its unprotected elements, was fair use. That case was about making games compatible with a console (“Sega Enters.”). Rebuilding how a game plays is a different purpose. A later law, from 1998, separately says no one may get around a technological measure that controls access to a protected work. Its reverse-engineering exception is limited to making programs work together (“Chapter 12”). I don’t know how either applies to a 1992 arcade game studied for a school project.
MY VIEW
Reverse engineering is how I found the real numbers, and I learned a lot from it. I think studying it is reasonable, but I can’t say the law clearly covers everything that was done, so I would rather say that than pretend.

WHO WROTE THE CODE

WHAT WAS DONE
The site says plainly that Claude Code wrote the code. The AI tools are cited on the Sources page, nearly all of the game’s commits are marked as co-authored by it, and all of it is public on GitHub.
THE QUESTION
If I didn’t type the code, in what sense did I make the game? The Copyright Office says prompts alone don’t make someone the author of AI output, though a person’s creative choices and changes can count (“Copyright Office Releases”).
MY VIEW
I made the decisions, so I think it’s fair to say I directed it, but I shouldn’t say I programmed it. Being honest about that matters more than sounding impressive.

AI-MADE ART AND SOUND

WHAT WAS DONE
SpriteCook made the first versions of the player art, and ElevenLabs made the sound effects. The game is a non-commercial school project, so ElevenLabs’ free plan was chosen, and it asks for a credit, which is on the Research page. Each file’s source and prompt are recorded in the repo.
THE QUESTION
Those tools learned from other people’s work. ElevenLabs names one licensed source and SpriteCook’s wasn’t checked. See Artists.
MY VIEW
I think it’s OK for a free school project if it’s credited, but I wouldn’t be as sure if I were selling the game.

THE SCHOOL’S NAME AND LOGO

WHAT WAS DONE
The Hogg Razorbacks shield is on the title screen and in the match, and the court uses the school’s colours and name.
THE QUESTION
The project’s notes said it was “worth confirming the school is happy” with the logo appearing publicly. They record my decision to use it, not an answer from the school.
MY VIEW
Ronin: ask the school, then say what they answered. Until then, the honest wording is: it’s my school and I meant it as a tribute, but that isn’t the same as permission.

PRIVACY AND REAL PEOPLE

WHAT WAS DONE
A group photo of the courts was kept out of the code because it shows students, and this site names no students except me. One real coach, who has agreed, is being added as a character. Their photos stay out of the code, but they were uploaded to SpriteCook as references.
THE QUESTION
Agreeing to be in a game isn’t necessarily agreeing to have your photos sent to an AI service. It depends on what they were told.
MY VIEW
Ronin: say whether the coach knew the photos would go to an AI tool. Then: I think people should get to choose this, especially students, which is why I kept them out.

OPEN QUESTIONS

  • Would real beginners do as well as my test robot at level 1? I would test with people who have never played it.
  • Does it feel like squash to people who play squash?
  • Why is the step from level 5 to 6 so big? Volleys start at level 6, but nothing has tested whether that’s the cause.
  • What does the arcade do at the moment of a racket hit that the decompiled code didn’t explain?
  • What did SpriteCook learn from?
  • What would Gaelco and the school say if they were asked? And what does my school district’s AI guidance say about a project like this?
  • Could I change the game without AI? That might be the best test of what I actually learned.

MY REFLECTION

It was really easy to do many hard steps that most people need coding for, and really easy to make changes to the code without searching for a long time.

I thought it would be really hard and I wouldn’t get it done, but it was way easier than I thought.

Giving every character their own racket and ability gave them a wide variety and made the game a lot more fun.

I was surprised by how easy it was to make the ball physics. The details for all the art and characters were the hardest.

Next time I would have more people test the game, to make more improvements and make the game more fun overall.
