RESEARCH &
METHODS

Before any code was written, I studied the 1992 arcade game the way a professional would: I read about it, ran it in an emulator with a robot player, and decompiled its code to find the exact rules. Then I tested my version against the real thing, frame by frame. This page explains each method, the tools, and the main ideas behind the game.

THE 1992 ARCADE GAME

Hogg Squash recreates how Gaelco's arcade game Squash plays. It uses none of the original's art, sound or code: these facts come from reading about the game, running it in an emulator and decompiling it (see Methods).

MADE BY
Gaelco S.A. of Barcelona, Spain, in June 1992. Directed by Luis Jonama; graphics by Esteve Polls and Xavi Fradera; software by Alexander Ekjanov.
THE MACHINE
An arcade board with a Motorola 68000 processor at 10 MHz, drawing a 320×240 picture about 57.4 times a second.
CONTROLS
A joystick to run and two buttons: HARD (fast drives) and SOFT (drops and low shots). The shot's angle comes from timing, not the joystick.
THE VIEW
A camera behind the glass back wall looks at the front wall, so you see your player from behind on a 3D court. A shadow and a landing arrow show where the ball is going.
SCORING
First to 9 points, and every rally scores a point (rally scoring). Each point you lose also costs one of your 5 “faults”, which work like lives.
PLAYERS
One player against the computer, or two head to head. There are 8 players from 8 countries, shown only by a portrait and a flag. No differences in speed or power were found in the code.
TOURNAMENT
Five rounds against harder and harder opponents: Qualify Game, 8th Final, Quarter-Final, Semi-Final and Final. Winning a round gives 2 extra faults.
LEFT OUT
Between points the original shows pin-up pictures of women, which the 2026 re-release covers with a flag. Hogg Squash leaves them out.
TODAY
Re-released on the Evercade Gaelco Arcade 2 cartridge (2022) and in the Gaelco Sports Collection (October 22, 2026).

METHODS

Each card says what the method is, who uses it in their jobs, and what it answered for this project. Ronin chose every method; the dated steps are in the build log.

1 · BACKGROUND RESEARCH

WHAT IT IS
Reading what others have already found: museum and archive records, reviews, the press kit and news of the re-release.
WHO USES IT
Game designers studying other games before they design (competitive analysis), historians and journalists.
WHAT IT ANSWERED
What the game is, who made it, its rules (first to 9, HARD and SOFT, 8 players, a tournament), what reviewers liked, and what to leave out. It also listed the questions only the real game could answer.

I wanted to know what made the original fun before deciding what to copy and what to change.

2 · EMULATION WITH A ROBOT PLAYER

WHAT IT IS
An emulator (MAME) is a program that pretends to be the 1992 arcade machine, so its game runs on a normal computer. A script in the Lua language played the game by itself and recorded what happened on every frame.
WHO USES IT
Game preservation projects that keep old games playable, and software testers who use scripts to play a game thousands of times (test automation).
WHAT IT ANSWERED
The scoring (rally scoring to 9), which button is HARD, how faults work, and how fast the ball goes: about 25 m/s toward the front wall and about 7 m/s coming back. The recordings, about 92,000 frames of game numbers, became the test data for the physics.

Reviews can be wrong or vague, but the real game can't be. Running it let me measure things instead of guessing.

3 · DECOMPILING

WHAT IT IS
Turning a program's machine code (the numbers a processor runs) back into code a person can read. This is a kind of reverse engineering. The tool was Ghidra.
WHO USES IT
Security researchers studying viruses, engineers making old and new systems work together, and game preservation “decomp” projects. Ghidra was made by the U.S. National Security Agency and released free in 2019.
WHAT IT ANSWERED
The exact ball physics, the shot tables (3 court zones × 12 kinds of contact × 2 buttons), how the computer player predicts the ball, and the difficulty tables. Gaelco had hidden parts of the code with encryption; 22 encrypted jump tables were decrypted to read it.

Watching the game shows what happens, but only the code shows the exact numbers that make it feel the way it does.

4 · GOLDEN TESTS

WHAT IT IS
Automatic tests that compare the new game's physics with frames recorded from the real one. The recordings are the “golden” answers. They run again after every change, so a change that breaks the match is caught at once (a regression test).
WHO USES IT
Software engineers who rewrite old programs and must keep them behaving the same, and emulator developers who test against real hardware.
WHAT IT ANSWERED
Whether the JavaScript physics really matches the arcade, and exactly where it doesn't. The numbers are in How close is the match? below.

“It feels right” isn't proof, because my memory of the arcade game could be wrong. A test gives a number anyone can check.

5 · COMPARING OPTIONS

WHAT IT IS
Listing what the project needs, checking what each option provides, weighing the trade-offs, then testing the leading choice before building on it.
WHO USES IT
Engineers (a trade study or decision matrix) and software teams, who record big choices in “decision records”.
WHAT IT ANSWERED
Whether to draw the game with plain HTML5 Canvas or the Phaser game framework. Phaser's physics is 2D only and can't do the arcade's rules, so it would only have helped with drawing. Plain Canvas was chosen and then proven by a small first build, which needed about 150 lines of setup code.

Picking a tool because it's popular could have cost days. Writing the needs down first made the choice clear.

6 · PLAY-TEST CHECKPOINTS

WHAT IT IS
Stopping at planned points to play the game, write down what feels wrong, and plan fixes before going on. Each round of build, test and fix is an iteration.
WHO USES IT
Game studios (play-testing and user research) and designers of apps and products (usability testing).
WHAT IT ANSWERED
The things only a person can judge: how the game feels, looks and sounds. There were 7 checkpoints, CP-A to CP-G, from October 18 to October 29, 2026, and each led to fix-up sessions. At CP-C, for example, Ronin lost 4–9 at the easiest level, so the computer player was made slower and clumsier at low levels.

A test can say the ball moves correctly, but not whether the game is fun. I had to do that part.

7 · MEASURING WIN RATES

WHAT IT IS
A robot “reference player”, built to play like a beginner, plays 1,000 rallies against the computer at each difficulty level, and a script counts the share of points it wins. Fixed random seeds make every run repeatable.
WHO USES IT
Game balance designers and data analysts, and chess-engine developers, who measure strength with thousands of automatic games (simulation).
WHAT IT ANSWERED
Whether levels 1 to 9 really go from easy to hard in even steps, measured the same way after each change. The reference player was checked against Ronin first: it won 34% of points against the old level 1, close to his 4–9 loss. The results are charted on Findings.

One person playing can't tell whether level 6 is a little or a lot harder than level 5. A thousand rallies can.

8 · VERSION CONTROL AND A PUBLIC LOG

WHAT IT IS
Git saves every change to the code as a “commit” with a date and a message, so any earlier version can be brought back. The commits are public on GitHub.
WHO USES IT
Almost every software team, and open-source projects. Scientists keep a dated lab notebook for the same reason.
WHAT IT ANSWERED
What changed, when and why. The build log on this site is made from the commits, and old versions of the game are still playable there.

It keeps an honest record. Anyone can check what was done and when, including which parts AI wrote.

HOW IT WORKS

Five ideas behind the game, in plain words first, then the name the field uses.

A 3D COURT ON A FLAT SCREEN

The game keeps track of the ball in three directions: across the court, how high, and how far from the front wall. The screen only has two. To draw it, the game imagines a camera 8.1 m behind the glass back wall and 4 m up, and works out where each point would appear on its picture. Things farther away are drawn smaller and closer to the middle.

That leaves a problem: a ball high up near you and a ball low down far away can land on the same spot on the screen. The ball's shadow, always drawn on the floor, tells you which.

Field terms: perspective projection with a pinhole camera; the ball's position is a 3D coordinate (x, y, z).

EXACT MATHS: THE SAME ANSWER EVERY TIME

The 1992 arcade's processor had no built-in way to do decimals, so its programmers stored numbers like 1.5 as whole numbers that count 65,536ths. Hogg Squash does its physics the same way. Whole-number maths has no rounding surprises, so the game gets exactly the arcade's numbers (which is why the tests can demand an exact match) and exactly the same result in every browser.

Getting the same result from the same start every time also matters for the computer player: its prediction of the ball (below) is only right if the real ball does exactly what the prediction did.

Field terms: 16.16 fixed-point numbers (16 bits for the whole part, 16 for the fraction); a program that always gives the same output for the same input is deterministic.

WHY THE FRONT WALL SLOWS THE BALL

In the recordings, a hard shot hit the front wall at about 25 m/s but came back at only about 7 m/s. That slowdown is what gives a player time to reach the ball. The code shows how: every bounce keeps a share of the ball's speed, from 68% for a slow ball down to 31% for a fast one, and the front wall counts each hit as if it were twice as fast. Harder levels make the ball a little “deader” still.

Field term: the share of speed a ball keeps after a bounce is its coefficient of restitution.

HOW THE COMPUTER PLAYER KNOWS WHERE TO RUN

After every hit the game sends out an invisible copy of the ball, the “ghost ball”. It runs the same physics three steps for every frame of the real ball, so it races ahead. Once the ghost has hit the front wall and then the floor, the game notes up to 4 places where a player could meet it. That prediction places the red landing arrow, and it is also the computer player's plan: after a short reaction delay, it runs to the earliest of those places it can reach in time.

Field terms: running a copy of the simulation ahead of time is a look-ahead or prediction; the computer player is the game's AI (artificial intelligence, the game-design meaning).

DESIGNING LEVELS 1 TO 9

The arcade has 20 hidden difficulty steps: 4 settings the arcade owner picks (Easy, Normal, Hard, Hardest), each with 5 skill levels that rise as you win. They aren't in order from easy to hard. For example, the computer reacts more slowly at Normal skill 0 (16 frames) than at Easy skill 1 (12 frames). Hogg Squash replaces them with levels 1 to 9 that get harder every step.

Each level sets the game's pace (ball speed, gravity, shot power, bounce) and how well the computer plays (how fast it reacts, how accurately it places itself and times its shots). Level 5 plays at the arcade's Normal pace. The computer's reaction time goes from 36 frames (about 0.6 s) at level 1 down to 1 frame at level 9.

Field terms: a difficulty curve; one that only ever goes up is monotonic. Changing it after testing is game balancing.

How the levels were checked, and how play-testing changed them, is on Findings.
