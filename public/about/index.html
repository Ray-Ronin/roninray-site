ABOUT THE
PROJECT

Hogg Squash is a squash game for the Hogg Middle School Razorbacks that plays in any web browser. It’s my GT Capstone project.

Start here. This page says why I made it, the question behind it and what I made. The other pages show how I researched it, how it was built, step by step, and what I found.

PLAY HOGG SQUASH

PURPOSE AND GOALS

Hogg has its own squash courts, and the Mission Success squash programme plays on them. I wanted to make a squash game for Hogg that anyone could play on a school computer, a phone or a home computer with nothing to install. I picked a 1992 arcade squash game as my model because it plays fast and simply, and I wanted my game to feel like it.

I also wanted to find out what AI tools can really do. Everyone is talking about AI writing code and making art, so I used it to build a whole game and kept a record of where it helped and where it needed me.

My goals:

  • Make a complete, finished game, not a demo: menus, a computer opponent with nine levels, two-player mode, sound and controls for keyboard, gamepad and touch.
  • Make it play like the arcade game, and check that with real measurements instead of guessing.
  • Make every picture and sound original, with nothing copied from the arcade game.
  • Keep a public record of every step, so anyone can see how it was made and what AI did.

MY QUESTION

ORIGINAL QUESTION
How can AI tools help one student research, design and build a complete video game?
REFINED QUESTION (proposed Nov 7, 2026)
What can AI tools do, and what still needs a person, when one student researches, designs and builds a complete video game?

Why it changed: my first question only asked how AI helps, so it already assumed the answer was “a lot”. While building the game, the play-tests kept showing things the AI couldn’t judge on its own, like whether the computer opponent felt fair or whether a shot felt right. The new question asks about both sides: what AI did, and what still needed me.

WHAT I MADE

Two things: a game (a designed product and digital tool) and this website, which is the project’s public process record.

THE GAME
Hogg Squash, a squash game that runs in any web browser, with nothing to install. Play alone against a computer opponent at one of 9 levels, or against a friend on one keyboard or two gamepads. On a phone or tablet, on-screen touch controls appear by themselves.
HOW IT PLAYS
It plays like Gaelco’s arcade game Squash (1992): first to 9 points, a HARD and a SOFT shot, and ball physics rebuilt from the original and tested against it. More about the 1992 game →
THE LOOK AND SOUND
All-original pixel art in Hogg red, black and white, with the school’s Razorbacks shield (used with approval) and a nod to the Mission Success squash programme that plays at Hogg. Original sound effects. Nothing is copied from the arcade game.
WHERE IT IS NOW
Version 1.0 was released on Oct 23, 2026. Since then I’ve added sound and a character select screen (version 1.15.0). A crowd and a tournament are planned next; see what’s up next.
THIS WEBSITE
Explains the project for someone reading it on their own: the research and methods, the build log of every step with playable old versions, what I found, the sources and a FAQ.
WHO IT’S FOR
My classmates, squash players at Hogg, and GT judges and teachers. The game is for anyone who wants to play; the website is for anyone who wants to know how it was made.

WHO DID WHAT

I research, plan, play-test and make the decisions; Claude Code writes the code.

ME
Chose the project and the arcade game to model it on. Directed the research. Decided what goes in the game and in what order. Play-tested it at six checkpoints (CP-B to CP-G) and decided what to fix after each one. Approved every use of the school’s logo and every spend on AI art and sound.
CLAUDE AND CLAUDE CODE
AI assistants from Anthropic. Claude helped research the arcade game and plan the build. Claude Code wrote the game’s code and this website, one short session at a time, and cleaned up the pixel art in Aseprite.
SPRITECOOK AND ELEVENLABS
AI generators. SpriteCook made the first versions of the player sprites and animations; ElevenLabs made the sound effects. Neither was given anything from the arcade game.
HOW THE WORK WAS CHECKED
Automated tests compare the game’s physics with recordings of the real arcade game. I play-tested every stage, and nothing went live without my OK.

MILESTONES

Dates come from the game’s commit history on GitHub. Each play-test checkpoint (CP) is where I played the game and decided what to change.

  1. Oct 18

  2. Oct 19
    Play-test 1 (CP-B) on two-player rallies, then a computer opponent (S5).
  3. Oct 20
    Play-test 2 (CP-C) against the computer at levels 1, 5 and 9 made the low levels gentler (S6b). Gamepads (S7) and the first pixel art (A1).

  7. Oct 24–31
    Sound. A sound engine (S13), sound effects (A6, S14), play-test 5 (CP-G, Oct 29) and its fixes (A6b, S14b).
